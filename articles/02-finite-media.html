Antonio de Nicolas, writing in 1976, put it in an interesting way

The human senses and their structure are also subject to change. Technology, for one, has changed human sensitivity and sense structure, thus changing the horizon of the human body. Neither the sensorial structure nor the human body of contemporary man is the same as they were a few years ago, though our biographical language has not yet learned to narrate this change.

He was talking about the twentieth century, but it feels like he is describing this year and will continue to make sense in the future as well.

Something changed in what we can feel, and it happened faster than our words for it. Take the simplest example. A beautiful image of a photograph or a painting that tells you something without you noticing: that a person went somewhere, waited for the light, and ruminated in solitude. You never thought about that; you just felt it.

Now, a beautiful image proves nothing, because anyone can make one in seconds, and the small admiration that used to come with it has nowhere to go. The above image would have evoked a feeling a few years ago, but now you know how it was generated and that it's not original. It doesn't evoke any sense of "wow".

The same has happened to abundance itself. Having everything was once a sensation. So the tiredness or the fatigue people describe is not a sign of weakness. It is a set of senses that no longer receive what they were built to receive, in a body that has not been told.

Nobody has the language for what infinite media has done or will do to us.

And that is what the finite media market is actually selling. Not paper, not vinyl, not seats. It is selling back the sensations that abundance switched off. The feeling that something took effort. The feeling that something is rare. The feeling of finishing.

We deleted the conditions for those feelings, and now we are buying them back one product at a time.

Losing the sense of self

It happened in stages, and each stage quietly took a different piece. The smartphone took the waiting and the body with it, turning the queue, the walk, and the train window into screen time. Then social media took our own unmediated read on what we like, and now the models are taking the first draft of every thought, which was where you used to find out what you actually believed.

Which is why the questions people are asking now are so oddly small.

What food do I like enough to choose the restaurant myself, what should I read out of the four hundred things I saved, and which twelve of these ten thousand photographs belong in an album? They sound trivial beside the large question, and they are not, because when you cannot answer what I am for, you can still answer which twelve.

Abundance is not automatically good. It can mean you finally go and find what is yours, or it can mean you never choose anything again and let the ranking do it for you. Therefore, you want to choose what is yours and then share it with others as a signal of status, an active life, or success, while others who do not know what they want fall victim to the trap of distribution. Almost everyone is living in this conflict. Isn't it?

The media is going through an economic problem

When the supply of something goes to infinity, its price goes to zero. It is the same thing that happened to the photograph, to recorded music, to the printed word, and it happens the same way every time. First, the thing becomes cheap. Then it becomes free. Then it becomes so free that having it means nothing at all.

But value does not vanish when price collapses. It moves. It moves to whatever the abundance cannot manufacture.

Look at what is left standing once anything can be generated on demand, and the list is oddly consistent. Proof of effort. Presence in a room. A body of taste. A fixed edition. A thing that ends. And a person you trust to choose for you.

Abundance is doing three things at once.

On the supply side, it is producing new formats, new content grammars, and new kinds of companies.

On the demand side, it is producing new scarcities, and people are already paying real money for them.

A third thing is happening between those two, and almost nobody is writing about it.

Abundance removes a constraint; everyone celebrates, and then, within a few years, the same constraint comes back and is sold as a feature. That middle movement is where most of the money will be made, because it is where people get charged for the habits they already have.

The Rise of Choiceless Awareness in the world of abundance

Choiceless awareness is what is left when attention stops selecting, and it is the difference between watching something and shopping through it. Finite media produces it by accident, because once you have paid the friction, there is nothing further to decide. AI is about attention-based prediction, and it picks what you should see next based on past data; if you remove that attention-led decision and take it into your control to do what you like, it is choiceless awareness.

1/ Why do I call this the finite media?

Anything that cannot be produced infinitely, that arrives with a constraint, and that asks something of you in return. The inconvenience is not a flaw; it is most of the product, because it is the only part that requires you to make a decision.

Every convenience of the last twenty years worked by quietly removing a decision, with autoplay choosing what came next and the ranking choosing what you saw. Taking one of those decisions back, deliberately and at some cost, is a small act of authorship, and a sense of self is assembled out of exactly these rather than out of large answers to large questions.

2/ The Two Traps: In Distribution & In Context

What has actually happened is that we have started living in distribution and in context, where everything on offer is calculated from what you have already accepted, and the frame you happen to be inside slowly stops feeling like one frame among several. I noticed it in food first, because quick commerce took the friction out of buying and, within a year, changed what I eat: I now pick from whatever arrives in ten minutes rather than from whatever I actually feel like. The same had been happening to what I read and watched for far longer, and I never caught it, because a recommendation engine can only offer what it predicts you will accept, and surprise is the one thing it is built to avoid.

3/ Choiceless Awareness helps you come out of the traps

What finite media really sells is a step outside that distribution, into something you truly feel like wanting, and not because it's available and coming to you as a choice. You are intentional about it and do not waver in your choices. You are aware that this is something you naturally want because it means a lot to you. When choices are gone, your awareness of being part of it is the only thing remaining.

You want this finite media without a choice because you deeply want to share it with your insiders or post it on the feed and stand out in the infinite media landscape.

The money is already showing up for this.

  • Fujifilm has expanded Instax film production three times in four years.
  • Life Four Cuts, the Korean photo booth chain, went from 12 billion won in revenue in 2020 to 49 billion in 2023.
  • US vinyl passed a billion dollars in revenue last year, and half of American buyers do not own a turntable.
  • Gen Z is four times more likely than older generations to visit an immersive experience, and 1.5 times more likely than the general population
  • 52 percent of Gen Z say they splurge on experiences when traveling

None of these are large markets, and none are trying to be; they are markets where prices are rising, which is the most reliable signal that people are buying something they cannot get anywhere else.

Something has to be given up before anything else can exist, and here, what gets given up is convenience, in return for the part of yourself that decides.

The New Trends of Finite Media

1. Tiny movies. Reels are content based on algorithm trends that are in distribution. The short-form wave has peaked, and now that anyone can make a clip, people want coherence instead. That results in a short video with an actual narrative. For ex: Short Vertical Films or Micro-documentaries. Tiny films made with a taste you really love to see. These will carry the highest return per rupee spent of anything in media or the new-age IPs. You will engage with tasteful content that has narratives. Creators are exploring IPs and micro-dramas on existing platforms, and the content is distributed in ways that help people feel more connected to it. Creation involves more friction than a typical no-brainer reel, and that friction limits supply while making it more valuable to the audience. The vertical serial is the first story grammar invented by distribution rather than film school, much like the sitcom was invented by the ad break.

2. Technology companies start studios. At the top of the market, Netflix has stood up a GenAI-native animation studio and paid a reported 587 million dollars for an AI filmmaking company, which tells you the model and the canon are moving into the same building. The more interesting version is below.

  • VCs are launching studios under new media arms for their portfolio companies.
  • Software startups are building in-house studios to do storytelling-led branding and communications.
  • Every consumer brand is quietly trying to make its own series on Instagram.

The real shift is the unit of brand media. It stops being a campaign and becomes the recurring, developing story you want to follow, because a campaign ends, but the story keeps getting followed.

3. Creative APIs. When production is cheap, the scarce asset is the shared world that many people can produce inside without breaking it. Games got here first. Roblox and Fortnite are now distribution layers, and brands have moved from one-off activations to permanent presences within them. Expect studios to publish creative endpoints the way software companies publish APIs, with a canon, a style guide, and rules about what you may not do to the characters.

The limits of Infinite Media are coming back

Every constraint the internet proudly removed is returning, priced. This is the act nobody is writing.

4. Streaming becomes network TV again. Weekly episode drops instead of the binge. Ad tiers. Live sport. Channels to surf inside the app. The industry spent fifteen years destroying the television schedule and is rebuilding it piece by piece. When all content is always available, the scarce thing is a schedule somebody else set for you. Infinite choice is not a feature; it is a tax on attention, and people will pay to have it removed.

5. Curation becomes distribution. As sources multiply, curation becomes the cheapest way to build an audience because you don't have to produce anything. Bring the material together, add a point of view, and engage the people who show up. This works the same way online and in a room. A curator is a schedule with a face. The differentiating skill set in curation is taste, and this sets you apart. An expert curator has good-quality taste, a point of view, and a community of like-minded people. This becomes a high-density channel for relevant brands to integrate themselves.

Curation and membership existed at the origin of media, not as gatekeeping, but as what made your participation mean something.

6. Online to Offline. Offprint prints the articles you saved and never read into a monthly magazine with your name on the cover. The mechanism matters more than the product. You already have the habit of saving content, and this sells you an ending to that habit. The extensions are obvious once you see it. Your Kindle and web highlights become an annual commonplace book, the object Readwise has never shipped. Some interesting ideas I would love to see here are: Your family WhatsApp becomes a yearbook; your kid’s first year of voice notes and photos becomes an annual, properly addressing the baby book market. Your Spotify year becomes a pressed record instead of a shareable card, because the card is exhausted and the object is the product. Your screenshots folder becomes a book of jokes, which is a document of taste.

The rule that separates the businesses from the graveyards is simple. You cannot sell someone a new habit. You can only sell them an ending for a habit they already have.

7. Design Maximalism ends Infinite minimalism slop. For a century, minimalism was the expensive choice because clean things took labor and restraint, quietly announcing you could afford it. That labor is now free, since smooth, competent output is the default of a machine, so restraint no longer signals anything at all. Minimalism at infinite scale is just AI slop.

What replaces it is not volume. Gen Z did not kill minimalism; they killed sameness, and what cannot be made at infinite scale is a human touch. A flaw is a limit made visible: the mark left exactly where something ran out, the ink, the hand, the time, the length of the run, which is why perfection is simply what unlimited supply looks like.

So expect density, texture, hand-set type, visible seams, and color that a person chose badly on purpose. Friction adds personality, and personality makes a design finite rather than infinite. The version that lasts is the imperfection that came from a real process, because a grain filter is one prompt away, and simulated flaws are only sameness in costume.

What people are paying for now

8. Presence. IRL storytelling grows because liveness is the only format that cannot be pirated, summarised or generated. The scarcity is you being in the room. Alongside it, slow media becomes a status symbol. Silent reading parties, phone-free screenings, deliberately long print. Attention is now what you visibly spend to prove you still have it.

9. Proof of a person. Comedy is about to have a very good decade, and not for sentimental reasons. Comedy is the genre in which the audience must believe that a person meant it. Timing, taboo, and specificity are exactly what models are worst at, and a joke that lands is evidence of a human in a way a beautiful image no longer is. Comedy also converts online into offline better than any other format, which is why stand-up keeps ending up on stage alongside consumer brands in India. Underneath it sits the unglamorous version of the same demand: provenance. I strongly believe the Made-by-human marks will be sold as ethics and bought as luxury in the near future.

10. Belonging. Niche communities will sustain unless they get too commercialized. Letterboxd, Substack, the good Discords. People are not leaving Instagram for these; they are adding a second, smaller feed, and the metric to watch is dual residency rather than defection. The death condition is always the same. The moment a platform starts monetizing its audience rather than serving its members, members can feel it within a month. Apps that remove the illusion of infinite supply, make it niche, and add friction to the experience will again be out of distribution and out of context, thereby making you more in tune with the people and events you truly care about.

11. Ownership. Physical media is coming back, but not in the way most trend pieces claim, and it is worth being precise because the skeptics are half right. There are three tiers. First, objects people genuinely use: print books, zines and indie magazines, board games, paper journals, e-ink devices, film photography. Nobody is going back to physical completely. They are asking the digital world for something to hold.

12. Finite Media led by patronage & subscriptions. A thousand true fans who actually pay, now that the rails finally exist. Superfan tiers, fan-financed films, community-owned IP, the annual bound volume sold to the top three per cent of a list. This is less a trend than the money layer underneath it, and it is what makes the smallest items on this list survivable as businesses. As you care more about what you really want, you will pay for creators or products that serve that need, making the creator sustainable and able to produce more such media for you. They promise you less content but more loved content, and not infinite content.

This is for another day

And the joke inside all of it is that every one of these finite products is discovered, sold, and evangelized through an infinite feed. The scroll is the shopfront for its own cure. Slowness, permanence, presence, proof of a person. These used to be the default conditions of culture, available to everyone, priced at nothing. They are on their way to becoming the premium tier.

So go back to the twelve photographs. Out of ten thousand, somebody chose those twelve, paid to have them printed, and bothered to hang them. The machines will make everything from here. What they cannot make is proof that a person took the trouble, and that is what people have started paying for.
